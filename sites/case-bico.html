Bico
Bicycle App

A companion app for urban cyclists — route planning, ride logging, and a community that doesn't feel like Strava.

Role

Solo Designer

Timeline

4 months · 2023

Tools

Figma, FigJam

Status

Concept · prototype

Overview

A bike app for people who ride, not train.

Bico started as a personal frustration. Every cycling app on the market is built for athletes — heart-rate zones, FTP curves, leaderboards. Most people on bikes are just commuting, running errands, or heading to the lake. They don't need a coach. They need a map, a logbook, and maybe a friend.

Bico is a calmer companion: route planning that prioritizes safety over speed, a ride log that looks like a photo journal, and a community that surfaces local rides instead of global rankings.

  • Quiet, photo-led ride journal
  • Bike-aware routing (lanes, surfaces, gradient)
  • Neighborhood ride feed instead of leaderboards
  • iOS native, designed for one-handed use
The Challenge

Designing for the 90% of cyclists who don't race.

The hardest part was resisting feature creep. Once you start sketching a cycling app, every piece of your brain says "but what about cadence sensors? Power meters? Routes by elevation?" Saying no was the design.

I ran six interviews with everyday riders before drawing a screen. The pattern was clear: they wanted to remember rides, share them lightly, and avoid scary streets. That's the entire product.

The Approach

Three core flows, designed for a stoplight.

Every primary action sits in the bottom third of the screen. Big tap targets. High contrast. The map is always one tap away — not buried behind a menu. The ride journal feels closer to a photo gallery than a data dashboard, with a single key metric and a generous space for notes.

  • One-handed first: every primary action reachable with thumb
  • Glanceable map UI for stop-and-go use
  • Photo-first ride journal with optional metrics
  • Calm color palette tested in direct sunlight

Outcome

A working prototype and a clearer philosophy.

Bico is currently a high-fidelity prototype tested with 12 riders over three weeks. It's not shipped yet, but the experience of designing it taught me to ruthlessly cut features. Every "wouldn't it be nice if…" got measured against the question: would this make the app heavier to open?

If the answer was yes, it didn't ship. That's the rule I now apply to every consumer product I work on.
