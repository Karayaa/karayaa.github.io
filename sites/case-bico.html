Bico
Bicycle Companion

Android Auto / CarPlay for the e-bike. Bico turns the smartphone into a safe on-board computer, with navigation, music, messages and bike data reduced to what you can handle while riding.

Role

Solo Designer

Timeline

5 months · Oct–Feb

Tools

Figma, FigJam

Status

Concept · prototype

Overview

One screen on the handlebar, built for riding.

Cyclists already use their phones on the bike to navigate, listen to music and podcasts, track workouts and answer messages. But those apps were never designed to be used while riding. At the same time, every e-bike brand ships its own on-board computer or its own app, and when that hardware breaks, it's expensive to replace.

Bico is a concept that brings both together. Like Android Auto or CarPlay in a car, it runs third-party apps in a reduced, glanceable interface. It also serves as the e-bike's display, so the clutter on the handlebar comes down to a single device. I followed the Design Thinking process from understanding the problem to a tested, clickable prototype.

  • App launcher for maps, music, fitness, messages and notes
  • Bike data (speed, assist mode, rides) in one place
  • Controllable by touch, voice and a handlebar HMI controller
  • Safety pauses, automatic light and dark mode, large UI
The Challenge

Phones on the handlebar are a distraction in traffic.

Using a smartphone on a bike is dangerous. Interfaces built for walking or sitting demand too much attention, and riders put themselves and others at risk.

The problem affects every kind of cyclist: commuters, people riding for sport and people riding for fun. E-bike owners have a second problem. The software is fragmented across brands, and switching to a new or used e-bike means learning a whole new system.

Research

Competitors, an industry expert and three personas.

I compared Android Auto, CarPlay, COBI.Bike, VanMoof and the Brose e-bike app on visual design, navigation, usability, features and intuitiveness. The car systems integrate third-party apps well but only work in a car. The bike apps are locked to one motor or brand, don't connect to other apps, or can't be used while riding at all.

To understand the technical side, I interviewed the product owner of the Brose e-bike app. Three findings shaped the concept:

  • Controlling the motor directly from the phone would need a wired connection; Bluetooth latency is too high
  • Third-party apps can be integrated through APIs, but riders will blame the bike maker when a service like Spotify breaks
  • The better approach: control the phone app through the e-bike's HMI instead of the other way around

A survey and three personas then covered the main user groups. Mia (19, student, leisure rider) needs safe navigation through busy city traffic. Ben (24, road cyclist) wants navigation and fitness tracking without switching between apps. Lea (28, daily commuter) wants podcasts and voice memos on her 45-minute ride.

Define

How might we make the phone safe to use while riding?

I turned the research into five "How might we" questions and defined a concrete answer for each one:

  • Safe use: large, reduced UI and forced pauses after long use without the HMI controller
  • HMI control: a tile layout with a highlight state that you navigate by up, down, left, right, select and back
  • Riding in the dark: light and dark mode switch automatically with ambient light, and dark mode can stay on permanently
  • Voice in the wind: the screen shows what was understood, so commands can be repeated, plus a tip to use headphones
  • Battery: a power-saving prompt after 30 minutes and screen brightness inside the app's own settings

The Approach

An app launcher that keeps the map in view.

Brainstorming produced the scope: map and navigation, bike info, tracking and fitness, hands-off entertainment, messages, memos and settings. I wrote use cases for the key moments. They covered first-time setup, planning a route, switching to dark mode and pairing a headset, replying to a message by voice, and changing a playlist mid-ride.

Everything starts from a central app launcher. When you leave the map, navigation stays visible as a small pop-up, so you can change a song without losing your route. Incoming messages appear as a pop-up on any screen, get read aloud by the assistant and can be answered with speech-to-text. You confirm the reply before it's sent.

  • Bike app with switchable speedometer modes and a ride history
  • Map routing with preferences such as avoiding main roads
  • Safety pause that briefly locks the screen during long use
  • Step-by-step onboarding for connecting the phone to the bike
Outcome

A tested prototype
and a clear iteration.

I tested the clickable Figma prototype as a scripted ride with music, a safety pause, a voice reply and dark mode. Testers liked the safety lock, the clean design and the easy switching between apps.

Next steps from the feedback: show more at once (map plus speed or music), alternative routes, a first-run tutorial and a landscape layout.

My takeaway: on a bike, every feature has to justify the attention it takes.
